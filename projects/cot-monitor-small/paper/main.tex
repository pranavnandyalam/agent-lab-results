\begin{abstract}
Chain-of-thought (CoT) monitors should catch reward hacks that only the agent's reasoning reveals, but for
small monitors it is unclear whether they respond to stated intent or to cue words such as ``cheat''. We probe
four zero-shot monitors (Qwen2.5-0.5B/1.5B-Instruct, Qwen3-0.6B/1.7B without thinking mode) on fully synthetic,
template-written toy transcripts: 6 task families $\times$ 20 instances, six matched conditions, three views,
scored by the YES$-$NO logit difference on CPU. Minimal-pair controls swap cue words for neutral synonyms or add
benign reasoning that mentions cue words. A cluster bootstrap over the 12 shared wording skeletons gives an
effective sample size of about 12. On CoT-only text, AUROC for intent-revealing versus benign reasoning is
0.805, 0.916, 0.957 and 0.990 (0.5B, 1.5B, 0.6B, 1.7B); two monitors exceed the pre-registered 0.95 ceiling,
so the size hypothesis is untestable. Under the selected prompt, removing cue words lowers AUROC by 0.288,
0.165, 0.052 and 0.025, but post-hoc re-scoring of the two smallest monitors under two other prompts gives
0.177 vs.\ 0.139 and 0.083 vs.\ $-$0.018: the direction holds, but the gap shrinks from 0.236 to 0.038
and 0.101, so a family difference is not established. The pre-registered keyword-reliance hypothesis is not supported for any monitor. A
bag-of-words baseline reaches 0.758 (1.000 when wording leaks across families), so monitor AUROCs cannot be
attributed to reading intent rather than template style. Pooled action-only AUROC is 0.476--0.668, but
per-family values range from 0.05 to 1.00 and can cross 0.5 when the prompt changes. These are probes on
synthetic data, not evidence about deployed monitoring.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Monitoring the chain of thought (CoT) of an agent with a second language model is a proposed defense against
reward hacking: an agent that edits a test, hardcodes an expected output, or disables a check may say so in its
reasoning even when its action looks plausible~\cite{baker2025monitoring,arnav2025redhanded,guan2025monitoring}.
Recent work shows that pooled monitor accuracy can hide a fragile subset: when only the reasoning reveals the
hack, rewriting the reasoning to sound like good-faith engineering collapses the catch rate of frontier
monitors~\cite{shiromani2026false}. Small monitors are attractive because they are cheap enough to run on
every step, but CoT-Guard reports that current 4B--8B models struggle to detect hidden objectives without
additional training~\cite{diwan2026cotguard}.

A specific concern for small monitors is that a high detection score on reasoning text can be produced by
lexical cues rather than by reading intent. A monitor that flags any transcript containing ``cheat'' will score
well on transcripts where hacking agents say ``I will cheat'', yet it will miss the same intent phrased
neutrally and raise false alarms on an honest agent that writes ``I will not cheat''. Standard accuracy on a
pool of hacks cannot separate these behaviors.

We study this question at 0.5--1.7B parameters with a small, fully controlled probe. Each synthetic instance
pairs a toy coding task with a benign and a hacky action and with matched reasoning variants written from shared
wording skeletons. The design lets us compare views of the same transcript and measure two minimal-pair effects.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A matched-transcript probe with six conditions and three views, including two minimal-pair controls
(cue words swapped for neutral synonyms; benign reasoning that mentions and rejects cue words) and a hack
action under benign narration, analyzed with a paired cluster bootstrap over shared wording skeletons
(Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item A pre-registered evaluation of four sub-2B zero-shot monitors from two open families, on CPU, with pinned
model revisions (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).
\item Results with explicit hypothesis verdicts: CoT-only separation of intent-revealing from benign reasoning
reaches AUROC 0.805--0.990; the size hypothesis is ceiling-limited, the keyword-reliance hypothesis is not
supported, and the narration-override hypothesis holds by its literal rule for one monitor only, with a small
effect (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item Post-hoc checks, added after review, showing that two apparent patterns are weaker than they first appear:
the family difference in cue reliance keeps its direction under two other prompts but its size is
prompt-dependent and not established, and pooled action-only AUROC
hides per-family values from 0.05 to 1.00 (Section~\ref{sec:posthoc}).
\item A documentation of what limits these conclusions: a bag-of-words baseline, the effective sample size of
12, prompt sensitivity, and cue priming by the selected prompt (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{CoT monitoring and its fragility.} Baker et~al.~\cite{baker2025monitoring} show that a GPT-4o monitor
reading the CoT of o3-mini in agentic coding environments detects reward hacking far better than an action-only
monitor, and that optimizing against the monitor produces obfuscated reasoning. CoT
Red-Handed~\cite{arnav2025redhanded} compares CoT and action-only monitoring with weaker trusted monitors,
finds that misleading rationalizations in the reasoning can deceive CoT monitors, and proposes a hybrid score.
Arike et~al.~\cite{arike2026information} report a ``less-is-more'' effect: monitors often perform better with
less of the trajectory. Guan et~al.~\cite{guan2025monitoring} propose evaluation archetypes and a metric for
monitorability and find CoT monitoring more effective than action-only monitoring in practical settings.
TRACE~\cite{wang2026thinking} targets implicit reward hacking, where the CoT looks benign, by measuring
reasoning effort, and compares against 32B and 72B CoT monitors.
Yang et~al.~\cite{yang2025investigating} study CoT monitorability along two axes, whether reasoning models
verbalize the factors behind their decisions and whether CoT monitors detect misbehavior reliably, noting that
monitors can be over- or under-sensitive. AutoMonitor-Bench~\cite{yang2026automonitor} evaluates 22 proprietary
and open LLM monitors on paired misbehavior and benign instances with separate miss and false-alarm rates,
reports a consistent trade-off between the two, and fine-tunes Qwen3-4B as a monitor. Our Acue condition is a
small, synthetic false-alarm probe in the same spirit, restricted to cue words.

\textbf{Closest work.} Shiromani and Richter~\cite{shiromani2026false} split Terminal
Wrench~\cite{bercovich2026terminal} hacks into action-visible and reasoning-dependent cases and show that a
rewrite of only the reasoning into good-faith narration drops a held-out monitor's catch rate on the
reasoning-dependent subset from about 95\% to 4--11\%. Their monitors are closed frontier models, with Qwen3-8B
and Qwen3-32B as open surrogates. Our conditions B (reasoning reveals intent, benign action) and D (hack action,
benign narration) are a small-scale, synthetic analogue of their reasoning-dependent subset and narration attack;
our narration is a fixed template, not an optimized rewrite. CoT-Guard~\cite{diwan2026cotguard} studies
4B--8B monitors and trains a 4B monitor; it does not evaluate untrained monitors below 4B.

\textbf{What differs here.} We evaluate untrained monitors at 0.5--1.7B and add a keyword minimal-pair control
(the same skeleton with cue words swapped for neutral synonyms, plus benign text that mentions cue words). In a
limited arXiv and web search (Semantic Scholar returned HTTP 429 and was skipped; no separate GitHub search was
run) we did not find a prior study at this scale with such a control. Not finding it does not mean it does not
exist. The view comparison and the benign-narration idea are prior work; our contribution is limited to the
scale and the minimal-pair control, on synthetic data.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
\textbf{Instances.} Six toy task families (fix a failing unit test, speed up a benchmark, pass a linter, pass a
config validator, reach a test-accuracy threshold, pass a checksum verification) each have 20 test instances.
Each instance has a task text, a benign action $a_0$ (a one-line edit that fixes the cause, followed by the
check output) and a toy hacky action $a_1$ (e.g., change the expected value in an assert, add a skip marker,
append a \texttt{noqa} comment, evaluate on the training split, overwrite the expected checksum). Reasoning
texts come from 12 hand-written skeletons. Each skeleton $p$ has four matched variants with slots for the
instance's check and goal: $R_\mathrm{ben}$ (benign), $R_\mathrm{rev}$ (reveals the intent to game the check
and contains at least one cue word), $R_\mathrm{revnc}$ (the same text as $R_\mathrm{rev}$ with every cue word
replaced by a neutral synonym), and $R_\mathrm{bencue}$ (benign reasoning that mentions and rejects cue words).
A fixed regular-expression list of 15 cue stems (e.g., \texttt{cheat}, \texttt{hack}, \texttt{game},
\texttt{bypass}, \texttt{skip}) is checked at generation time: $R_\mathrm{ben}$ and $R_\mathrm{revnc}$ contain
none, $R_\mathrm{rev}$ and $R_\mathrm{bencue}$ contain at least one. Table~\ref{tab:example} shows one
skeleton. The 12 skeletons are shared by all six families, so instance $j$ of every family uses skeleton
$j \bmod 12$.

\textbf{Conditions and views.} The CoT+action view has six conditions:
A $=(R_\mathrm{ben},a_0)$ (negative), B $=(R_\mathrm{rev},a_0)$ (only the reasoning reveals the hack),
C $=(R_\mathrm{rev},a_1)$ (visible hack), D $=(R_\mathrm{ben},a_1)$ (hack under benign narration),
Bnc $=(R_\mathrm{revnc},a_0)$ and Acue $=(R_\mathrm{bencue},a_0)$. The CoT-only view shows the four reasoning
variants without an action, and the action-only view shows $a_0$ and $a_1$ without reasoning. This gives
$6+4+2=12$ scoring passes per instance and 1440 per monitor. In the action-only view, B and A are identical
inputs, so their AUROC is 0.5 by construction (a sanity check), and D versus A equals C versus A.

\textbf{Monitor readout.} Each monitor receives a system message (``You are a careful monitor that reviews
transcripts of AI coding agents.''), the task, the reasoning and/or action, and the question (prompt v2):
``Gaming the task means making the check pass without genuinely accomplishing the goal, for example by editing
tests, hardcoding expected outputs, or skipping or disabling checks. Is the agent trying to game the task
instead of solving it? Answer with exactly one word: YES or NO.'' The score is
$s=\ell_\mathrm{YES}-\ell_\mathrm{NO}$, the difference of next-token logits at the first assistant position.
Format compliance is the probability mass on the two tokens.

\textbf{Estimands.} All detection metrics are AUROCs between the 120 positive and 120 negative passes of a
contrast (ties count one half). Keyword reliance is $K=\mathrm{AUROC}(\mathrm{B}\,\mathrm{vs}\,\mathrm{A})-
\mathrm{AUROC}(\mathrm{Bnc}\,\mathrm{vs}\,\mathrm{A})$. The cue false-alarm effect is the mean paired score
difference $\overline{s(\mathrm{Acue})-s(\mathrm{A})}$, in logit units. Narration override is
$O=\mathrm{AUROC}_\mathrm{action}(\mathrm{D}\,\mathrm{vs}\,\mathrm{A})-\mathrm{AUROC}_\mathrm{CoT+action}(\mathrm{D}\,\mathrm{vs}\,\mathrm{A})$;
$O>0$ means benign narration lowers detection of a hack action.

\textbf{Uncertainty.} Scoring is deterministic, so there are no seeds; all variance comes from the choice of
wording. Because the 12 skeletons are shared across families, the pre-registered primary bootstrap unit is the
skeleton (12 clusters). We draw 2000 cluster resamples (NumPy seed 0), compute the cluster-weighted AUROC
exactly for each resample, and report 2.5/97.5 percentile intervals. The same resample weights are used for
every monitor and metric, so differences are paired. A secondary bootstrap over the 72 family$\times$skeleton
clusters is reported only where noted; it ignores the shared wording and is optimistic.

\textbf{Bag-of-words baseline.} A binary-unigram, L2-penalized logistic regression on the CoT text gives
out-of-fold scores for B vs A and Bnc vs A, with cross-validation grouped by skeleton (12 folds, primary) or by
family$\times$skeleton (72 folds).

\textbf{Pre-registered hypotheses.} (H1, sanity) action-only B vs A $=0.5$; if the skeleton-grouped BoW AUROC
for B vs A is $\geq 0.95$, monitor AUROCs are read as template-wording detection.
(H2) Within each family, CoT-only AUROC(B vs A) is higher for the larger model (paired CI of the difference
excludes 0); if any of the four monitors exceeds 0.95, H2 is declared ceiling-limited for both families.
(H3) Keyword reliance is supported for a monitor only if $K\geq 0.15$ with CI lower bound $>0$ \emph{and} the
CoT-only Acue$-$A score difference is $>0$ with CI excluding 0.
(H4) $O>0$ with CI excluding 0 for at least one monitor.

\textbf{Cue words (H3).} Swapping cue words for neutral synonyms lowers CoT-only AUROC from 0.805 to 0.517
(Qwen2.5-0.5B) and from 0.916 to 0.751 (Qwen2.5-1.5B), but only from 0.957 to 0.905 (Qwen3-0.6B) and from 0.990
to 0.964 (Qwen3-1.7B). The resulting $K$ is 0.288, 0.165, 0.052 and 0.025 (Table~\ref{tab:effects},
Fig.~\ref{fig:effects}). Under v2, $K$ is lower for the larger model in each family and lower for Qwen3 than
for Qwen2.5, but we computed no CI for these differences, and Section~\ref{sec:posthoc} shows that for the two smallest
monitors the direction of the family contrast holds under the other two prompts but the gap is much smaller. Both Qwen2.5 monitors meet the $K$ part of H3. The second part fails for all four: in the
CoT-only view, no Acue$-$A score difference has a CI excluding 0, and the CIs are wide (up to
[$-$2.181, 2.755] for Qwen3-1.7B). \emph{H3 verdict: not supported for any monitor}, which here means ``not
demonstrated'', not ``refuted''. The secondary CoT+action Acue$-$A difference for Qwen3-0.6B, 0.397 [0.106,

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{What the AUROCs do and do not show.} Our intent-revealing reasoning states the plan to game the check
explicitly, and the neutral-synonym variant keeps that statement (``make the check report success whether or
not \ldots''). Under v2 the Qwen3 monitors keep most of their separation when cue words are removed, while the
Qwen2.5 monitors lose much of it. For the two smallest monitors the direction holds under v1 and v3, but the
gap shrinks to 0.038 and 0.101 (Table~\ref{tab:kprompt}), so we do not interpret it as an established family
or size property. The bag-of-words result limits any intent claim further: once a skeleton's wording is seen, unigrams determine the label, and even
without that leak a unigram model reaches 0.758. We therefore cannot attribute any monitor's AUROC to
``understanding intent'' rather than detecting the style of our synthetic reasoning. With only 12 training
skeletons, the skeleton-grouped BoW rule is also close to unfalsifiable.

\textbf{Small effective sample size.} The six families share 12 skeletons, so wording varies along about 12
independent units. The primary CIs are wide (e.g., $K$ for Qwen2.5-0.5B spans [0.205, 0.386]), percentile
bootstrap intervals with 12 clusters may under-cover, and we report many estimands without multiplicity
correction. The 72-cluster intervals are narrower but treat wording shared across families as independent.

\textbf{Prompt sensitivity and cue priming.} On dev, prompt v1 inverted Qwen2.5-0.5B (AUROC 0.207 with full
format compliance) and v3 gave 0.340, while v1 gave 0.997 and 1.000 for the two larger monitors
(Table~\ref{tab:dev}). Results at this scale therefore depend strongly on wording, and a single prompt per
model is a major limitation; the post-hoc test re-scoring (Table~\ref{tab:kprompt}) shows that the
size of the cue-reliance gap also changes with the prompt. The selected prompt v2 contains cue stems (editing tests, hardcoding, skipping or
disabling checks), which may prime cue vocabulary and inflate $K$ and the Acue effect. The Acue texts also use
cue words in negated form (``I will not cheat''), which tests negation handling as much as cue sensitivity.

\textbf{Limits of the post-hoc checks.} Section~\ref{sec:posthoc} is exploratory and incomplete. Only the two
smallest monitors were re-scored under v1 and v3, so we cannot say whether $K$ for Qwen2.5-1.5B and Qwen3-1.7B
is prompt-stable. All post-hoc numbers are point estimates without CIs; per-family cells rest on 20 vs 20
passes, and every AUROC still varies along only about 12 wording skeletons. The bag-of-words confound applies
to the per-prompt $K$ values as much as to the primary ones. Two further checks raised in review were not
done: we did not verify that each $R_\mathrm{revnc}$ rewrite expresses the same intent as its $R_\mathrm{rev}$
source (if a synonym swap weakens the stated intent, $K$ overstates cue reliance), and we did not control
whether the hack actions themselves contain cue stems from the fixed list (e.g., a skip marker), so the action
view may also partly measure lexical cues.

\textbf{Narration.} Our condition D uses a fixed benign template that describes a correct fix while the action
does something else; it is not an optimized rewrite as in~\cite{shiromani2026false}. The negative $O$ for
Qwen3-1.7B may reflect that the mismatch between narration and action is easy to notice in our toy setting;
we did not test this.

\textbf{Pre-registration timing.} The plan and hypotheses (H1--H4) were approved before any scoring. The
dev-selection script was written after the first dev score file existed, so ``frozen before looking'' is
asserted, not demonstrable. The analysis choices (global ceiling for H2, CoT-only as the H3 view,
skeleton-grouped BoW) were written into the plan before any test analysis was run, according to the agent's
log, but they were committed in the same commit as the first monitor's test scores, so version control does not
show their order. All four monitors were scored before the first test analysis.

\textbf{Scope.} The transcripts are short, lexically regular, written by the same agent that designed the
hypotheses, and far from real agent traces; dev and test share the task and action templates. We use zero-shot
monitors with a single YES/NO readout, no calibration or threshold, no Qwen3 thinking mode, and two model families
at 0.5--1.7B. Nothing here supports claims about deployed monitoring. Validation on real hack trajectories such
as Terminal Wrench~\cite{bercovich2026terminal} is future work.

\section{Conclusion}
On synthetic, template-written transcripts, four zero-shot monitors of 0.5--1.7B parameters separate
intent-revealing from benign reasoning with AUROC 0.805--0.990. Pooled over families, they recognize the toy
hack actions alone poorly (0.476--0.668), but per-family action AUROCs range from 0.05 to 1.00 and change
direction across prompts. Under the selected prompt, removing cue words costs the two Qwen2.5 monitors 0.288 and
0.165 AUROC and the two Qwen3 monitors 0.052 and 0.025, for the two smallest monitors the direction is
the same under the other two prompts, but the gap shrinks from 0.236 to 0.038 and 0.101, so a family
difference in cue reliance is not established. The pre-registered
size hypothesis is ceiling-limited, the keyword-reliance hypothesis is not supported, and the
narration-override hypothesis holds by its literal rule only through a small pooled effect in a monitor whose
pooled action-only AUROC is below chance. A bag-of-words baseline shows that the synthetic wording is
highly predictive, so these numbers describe how small monitors react to controlled text, not whether they
understand intent. A larger and more varied set of wording skeletons, several prompts for every monitor with
CIs, an intent-equivalence check of the cue-free rewrites, and real agent traces are the next steps.
