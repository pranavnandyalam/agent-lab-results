\begin{abstract}
Small open language models are attractive as cheap pairwise judges, but does their verdict depend on the
criterion asked? We test this by negation: each pair is judged under ``Which
response is better?'' and ``Which response is worse?'', in both presentation orders. We evaluate four Qwen
judges (Qwen2.5-0.5B/1.5B-Instruct, Qwen3-0.6B/1.7B; thinking off; next-token A-vs-B logit readout; CPU) on 300
RewardBench pairs, 217 of which are HumanEvalPack code pairs. Under the argmax readout, a correct reversal
occurs in 0, 0, 0 and 1 of 300 pairs. Our pre-registered ``position-locking'' hypothesis (same letter in all four judgments) is
supported for the two smallest judges (1.000 and 0.890), but a letter-biased null shows this is largely implied by
their letter rates. A
post-hoc letter-calibrated analysis that averages the A-minus-B logit over both orders shows the actual failure:
the pair-dependent logit shift under ``worse'' is strongly positively correlated with the shift under
``better'' (Pearson $r$ = 0.95, 0.63, 0.90, 0.63; same sign in 0.71--0.92 of pairs), so the question barely
changes which response is favored. The 0.95 comes from Qwen2.5-0.5B, which always answers ``A'' and whose shift is
tiny relative to its letter offset; the three other judges give 0.63--0.90. Only Qwen3-1.7B shows clear content
signal (calibrated ``better'' accuracy 0.81 vs.\ 0.50--0.61), yet it does not reverse. The pattern holds on the 83 non-code pairs ($r$ 0.57--0.95) and on high-format-compliance
pairs. Generation-based readouts, thinking, and judges above 2B were not tested.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Pairwise LLM judges are widely used to compare model outputs~\cite{zheng2023judging}, and their position bias
is well documented~\cite{wang2023fair,shi2025judging,norman2026reliability}. Small open models (below 2B
parameters) are attractive judges because they run on commodity CPUs, and recent work evaluates Qwen3 models
as small as 0.6B as judges on RewardBench~\cite{jayarao2025explicit}. A basic precondition for any judge is
that its verdict depends on the criterion it is asked about. Accuracy on a ``which is better?'' prompt cannot
establish this, and an order-swap test detects position bias but not whether the judge reads the question.

We probe criterion conditioning by negation, a property known as negation invariance in the logical-consistency
literature~\cite{liu2024aligning}: a judge that prefers the chosen response under ``better'' should prefer the
rejected one under ``worse''. Asking both questions in both orders gives four judgments per pair. Our first
analysis, which was pre-registered, classified each pair by the four argmax verdicts and concluded that the
smallest judges are ``position-locked''. Referee feedback showed that this conclusion was driven by the
readout: strongly letter-biased judges produce the same letter four times regardless of what they prefer. This
revision therefore separates the letter prior from content preference by averaging over presentation orders,
and finds a pair-dependent shift that is mostly the same under both questions.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A four-judgment negation design with two readouts: a pair-level argmax taxonomy and a letter-calibrated
order-averaged logit difference that removes a constant letter offset (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item An evaluation of four sub-2B Qwen judges (thinking off) on 300 RewardBench pairs with pinned model and
data revisions (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).
\item The finding, for these judges and this sample, that the order-averaged logit shift under ``worse'' is
strongly positively correlated with that under ``better'' (criterion-blindness), robust to excluding code pairs
and low-mass passes (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item A correction of our own pre-registered framing: under a letter-biased null the position-locking
hypothesis is nearly implied by letter marginals, so argmax taxonomies can misattribute criterion-blindness to
position bias (Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:disc}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Negation and logical consistency of judges.} Liu et al.~\cite{liu2024aligning} propose measuring
logical preference consistency of LLM judgments by transitivity, commutativity and negation invariance (whether
a model inverts its judgment when the relation is negated), evaluate open models from Phi-3-mini to 13B and two
API models, and introduce the REPAIR data augmentation. Our ``better'' vs.\ ``worse'' test is an instance of
negation invariance; we add order swaps, sub-2B judges and a letter-calibrated readout. GRP~\cite{song2025grp}
asks strong closed judges which answer is \emph{worse} as a prompting method and reports improved consistency
on JudgeBench. Bagaria et al.~\cite{bagaria2026rubric} reverse the rubric criterion in pointwise judging with
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and report desired verdict flips of 16.8\% and 32.2\% (two candidate models) under criterion
reversal. PRIN~\cite{ahn2025prin} contrasts ``which are correct?'' with ``which are incorrect?'' in
multiple-choice settings with models of 8B parameters and larger. Elkins and Chun~\cite{elkins2026framing}
audit negated vs.\ affirmative framings of moral dilemmas in 16 models and report swings of up to 76
percentage points for small (1--4B) models; their task is stance-taking, not response comparison.
JudgeSense~\cite{bellibatlu2026judgesense} measures judge sensitivity to prompt rewording more broadly.

\textbf{Position bias and hidden signal.} Zheng et al.~\cite{zheng2023judging} report position and verbosity
bias; Wang et al.~\cite{wang2023fair} propose balanced-position calibration, which our order-averaged readout
resembles; Shi et al.~\cite{shi2025judging} study position bias across 15 judges; Norman et
al.~\cite{norman2026reliability} evaluate 21 judges from nine providers and report high test-retest
reliability coexisting with severe position bias in two production-deployed judges. Mukherjee and Sitaram~\cite{mukherjee2026opening} study 64
open-weight judges and report that on LLMBar~\cite{zeng2024llmbar} the verdicts of 50 judges agree with human
labels only 0.456 of the time even after averaging both orders, while probes on activations recover much more
signal. Our calibrated analysis makes a related, simpler observation at the output layer: the argmax verdict of
a letter-biased judge hides a content preference that the logit difference still carries.

\textbf{Negation in language models.} Pretrained LMs often fail to distinguish negated from affirmative
probes~\cite{kassner2020negated}, and larger LMs can perform worse on negated prompts~\cite{jang2022negated}.

\textbf{Difference from prior work.} Negation invariance of judges~\cite{liu2024aligning} and reversed-goal
prompting~\cite{song2025grp} are established ideas, so our contribution is narrow: applying the negation test
in both orders to four judges below 2B parameters, and showing that a letter-calibrated readout changes the
diagnosis from position-locking to criterion-blindness. Our literature scan used arXiv and web search only;
we do not claim to be first.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}
\textbf{Prompt.} Each judgment uses the model's chat template with a single user turn containing the user
request, ``[Response A]'' and ``[Response B]'', a question, and ``Answer with a single letter: A or B.'' The
questions are \emph{better} (``Which response is better?''), \emph{W1} (``Which response is worse?'', primary
negation), \emph{W2} (``Which response should be rejected?'') and \emph{length} (``Which response is longer?'',
control). For Qwen3 we pass \texttt{enable\_thinking=False}.

\textbf{Readout.} One forward pass; the score $s$ is the difference of next-token logits of the single tokens
``A'' and ``B''; the argmax verdict is the letter with the higher logit. The probability mass on \{``A'', `` A'',
``B'', `` B''\} is recorded as a format-compliance measure. Inference is deterministic.

\textbf{Four judgments per pair.} For each pair (chosen $C$, rejected $R$) we ask \emph{better} and W1, each with
$C$ shown as A (order \emph{cf}) and with $R$ shown as A (order \emph{rf}).

\textbf{Argmax taxonomy (pre-registered).} Each pair receives the first matching category: (1)~\emph{correct
reversal}: $C$ in both orders under \emph{better} and $R$ in both orders under W1; (2)~\emph{position-locked}:
the same letter in all four judgments; (3)~\emph{criterion-blind}: the same response in all four; (4)~\emph{reversed-consistent};
(5)~\emph{other}. Under a uniformly random judge the shares are 1/16, 2/16, 2/16, 1/16 and 10/16.
\emph{Position-consistent accuracy} is the share of pairs with $C$ chosen in both orders under \emph{better}
(the conditioning subset, size $n_\mathrm{cond}$); the \emph{conditional flip rate} is the share of that subset
for which W1 selects $R$ in both orders.

\textbf{Letter-calibrated readout (post hoc).} For each pair and question $q$,
$d_q = (s_q^{cf} - s_q^{rf})/2$. If the judge adds a constant offset to the ``A'' logit, it cancels; $d_q>0$
means the judge favors $C$ \emph{as the answer to $q$}. A criterion-following judge has $d_\mathrm{better}>0$
and $d_\mathrm{W1}<0$ on most pairs, i.e.\ a negative correlation; a criterion-blind judge, which favors the same
response whatever the question, has a positive correlation. We report Pearson $r(d_\mathrm{better},
d_\mathrm{W1})$, the same-sign share, calibrated accuracy (share with $d_\mathrm{better}>0$) and calibrated
reversal (share with $d_\mathrm{better}>0$ and $d_\mathrm{W1}<0$), with 95\% bootstrap CIs over pairs (2000
resamples, seed 0).

\textbf{Letter-biased null.} For each judge, an independent-letter judge with the observed per-question
``A'' rates $p_b$ (better) and $p_w$ (W1) gives all four judgments the same letter with probability
$p_b^2p_w^2+(1-p_b)^2(1-p_w)^2$ and has position-consistent accuracy $p_b(1-p_b)$.

\textbf{Hypotheses (pre-registered).} H1: the conditional flip rate rises with size within each family (paired
bootstrap, Bonferroni-adjusted 97.5\% CIs); declared inconclusive if any $n_\mathrm{cond}<30$. H2: among
non-correct-reversal pairs, the position-locked share exceeds 0.5 (95\% CI lower bound $>0.5$), for Qwen2.5-0.5B
and Qwen3-0.6B. The calibrated readout and letter-biased null were added after review and are not
pre-registered. A development-set A/B-mass exclusion check ($<0.5$) was run for Qwen2.5-0.5B only (20 pairs, mean
mass 0.9998), a protocol deviation; on the evaluation set all judges have mean mass $\ge 0.745$.

\textbf{H2 is close to tautological.} The letter rates explain most of the argmax picture. Per-question ``A''
rates (better/W1) are 1.00/1.00, 0.20/0.01, 0.94/0.94 and 0.34/0.12 (Fig.~\ref{fig:letters}). An
independent-letter judge with these rates gives all four judgments the same letter in 1.00, 0.63, 0.79 and 0.34
of pairs; observed: 1.00, 0.71, 0.89 and 0.43 (Table~\ref{tab:cal}). Against the uniform random judge (0.125)
H2 is therefore nearly guaranteed for any strongly letter-biased judge, and its support tells us about letter
bias, not about the criterion. Likewise, position-consistent accuracy under the letter-biased null is 0.000,
0.159, 0.053 and 0.224, close to the observed 0.000, 0.103 and 0.050 for the three smaller judges; only
Qwen3-1.7B (0.430) exceeds what its letter rates predict.

\textbf{Calibrated readout: criterion-blindness.} Table~\ref{tab:cal} and Fig.~\ref{fig:cal} show the
letter-calibrated analysis. For all four judges, $d_\mathrm{W1}$ is strongly \emph{positively} correlated with
$d_\mathrm{better}$: $r$ = 0.95 [0.92, 0.97], 0.63 [0.51, 0.73], 0.90 [0.87, 0.93] and 0.63 [0.55, 0.71]. The
same sign occurs in 0.92, 0.71, 0.87 and 0.76 of pairs, and every CI lower bound is above 0.5 (0.88, 0.66, 0.82,
0.71). A criterion-following judge would show a negative correlation. Thus, once the letter offset is removed, each
pair still produces a shift $d$ whose sign is mostly unchanged by the question; the question mainly moves the
letter prior. This shift need not be a quality judgment: calibrated ``better'' accuracy is 0.61, 0.50, 0.57 and
0.81, i.e.\ near chance for the three smaller judges, and only Qwen3-1.7B shows clear content signal under
\emph{better}, which it does not invert under W1. For Qwen2.5-0.5B, $d$ is also tiny compared with the letter
offset: the median $|d_\mathrm{better}|$ is 0.047 logits against a mean order-averaged A-minus-B score of 2.80
(Qwen3-1.7B: median $|d_\mathrm{better}|$ 5.4). Its $r=0.95$ therefore shows that small residual shifts are
reproduced across questions, not that this judge has a coherent preference between the responses. The calibrated-reversal shares (0.06 to 0.22) are the tail
where $d$ changes sign; we defined no null for them and do not interpret them as evidence of reversal. Under
the argmax taxonomy the three smaller judges look position-locked rather than criterion-blind (criterion-blind
shares 0.000 to 0.113 for all four judges in Table~\ref{tab:main}) because a large letter offset dominates the
argmax verdict.

\textbf{Robustness.} (i)~\emph{Sample composition}: the correlation is $0.57$--$0.95$ on the 83 non-HEP pairs
and $0.58$--$0.96$ on the 217 HEP pairs (Table~\ref{tab:cal}). Calibrated ``better'' accuracy is lower on
non-HEP pairs (0.43, 0.53, 0.48, 0.61) than on HEP pairs (0.68, 0.48, 0.61, 0.88), so the content signal of
Qwen3-1.7B is stronger on code pairs, but criterion-blindness is present in both subsets. (ii)~\emph{Format compliance}:
restricting to pairs with A/B mass $>0.9$ on all four passes ($n$ = 300, 288, 286, 140) gives $r$ = 0.95, 0.60,
0.91, 0.63. For Qwen3-1.7B, whose mean mass is 0.745 and 25.2\% of whose better/W1 passes have mass below 0.5,
this filter keeps less than half of the pairs. (iii)~\emph{Not done}: we did not check the logit readout
against generated verdicts.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:disc}
\textbf{Interpretation.} On this sample, the pair-dependent part of the four judges' A-minus-B logit barely
depends on whether they are asked which is better or which is worse; the question wording mostly moves the letter
prior. This is criterion-blindness in the sense that whichever response the judge leans towards is insensitive to
the criterion; for three of the four judges that lean is close to chance with respect to the RewardBench label.
Our earlier, pre-registered interpretation that the smallest judges are ``position-locked'' and that the
negation test is therefore uninformative for them was wrong: it followed from an argmax readout that a large
letter offset dominates. The positive correlation may reflect any pair-level feature the judges respond to
(quality, length, surface form); $d$ cannot say which, only that it is not inverted by the negated question.
For practitioners, the lesson is methodological as much as substantive: argmax verdicts of letter-biased
judges can hide graded preferences, and an order-averaged logit difference is a cheap check that changes the
diagnosis.

\textbf{Scope.} The claim is limited to four Qwen judges up to 1.7B, a next-token argmax A/B readout (and its
order-averaged logit difference), thinking disabled, one prompt template, and a 300-pair RewardBench
subsample in which 217 pairs are HumanEvalPack code pairs and responses are at most 150 tokens. We make no claim
about larger judges, reasoning-enabled judges~\cite{jayarao2025explicit}, or generated verdicts.

\textbf{Threats to validity.}
(i)~\emph{Post-hoc analysis}: the calibrated readout, the letter-biased null and the subset analyses were
added after review; they were not pre-registered. Their CIs are descriptive, not tests with multiplicity
control.
(ii)~\emph{Calibration model}: $d$ removes a constant additive letter offset; order effects that interact with
content are not removed. Content-by-slot interactions (e.g.\ an ``A'' logit that depends on the length of the text in
slot A) enter $d$ identically under both questions and would push $r$ upward without any criterion-independent
preference for a response.
(iii)~\emph{What $d$ measures}: $d$ is a pair-dependent shift that the question mostly leaves unchanged, not
necessarily a quality judgment. Calibrated ``better'' accuracy is near chance (0.50--0.61) for three judges; only
Qwen3-1.7B (0.81) shows clear content signal. For Qwen2.5-0.5B, $d$ is tiny relative to the letter offset (median
$|d_\mathrm{better}|$ 0.047 vs.\ mean offset 2.80), so its $r=0.95$ is not evidence of a coherent preference.
(iv)~\emph{Readout}: no generation-based check was run; for Qwen3-1.7B the A/B mass is low on a quarter of
passes, and the mass$>$0.9 subset ($n=140$) is a selected sample.
(v)~\emph{Sample}: code-heavy, near-identical HEP pairs; the non-HEP subset is small ($n=83$); chat has 12
pairs.
(vi)~\emph{Hypotheses}: H1 could not be evaluated as designed (tiny conditioning subsets); H2 is supported as
pre-registered but, given the letter rates, nearly tautological.
(vii)~\emph{Protocol deviation}: the development-set mass check was run for one judge only.
(viii)~\emph{Novelty}: the related-work scan used arXiv and web search only.

\textbf{What was not done.} No judge of 3--4B parameters and no thinking-enabled condition were run (budget
rule; see Section~\ref{sec:setup}); no generation-based readout; no calibrated re-analysis of the W2 and
length controls; no prompt-wording search beyond W1/W2.

\section{Conclusion}
Asked ``which response is worse?'', four Qwen judges between 0.5B and 1.7B parameters (thinking off,
A/B logit readout) almost never reverse their ``better'' verdict under argmax (0, 0, 0 and 1 of 300 pairs).
The pre-registered diagnosis of position-locking does not hold up: it is largely implied by the judges' letter
rates. After removing the letter offset, the pair-dependent logit shift under ``worse'' is strongly positively
correlated with that under ``better'' ($r$ = 0.63 to 0.90 for the three judges that do not always answer ``A'';
0.95 for Qwen2.5-0.5B, whose shift is tiny relative to its letter offset), including on non-code pairs and
high-mass pairs, so these judges are criterion-blind on this sample. Only Qwen3-1.7B shows clear content signal
under ``better'' (calibrated accuracy 0.81). Whether generated verdicts, thinking, or larger judges change this
is open.
